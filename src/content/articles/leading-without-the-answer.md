Anyone who leads people eventually faces the same moment: someone looks at you for an answer, and you don't have one.

It can feel like exposure. In truth, it's one of the most important tests of leadership.

## The pressure to seem certain

We often confuse leadership with certainty. If we hesitate, we assume trust will fall. So we bluff, over-promise or decide too quickly, and the problems are bigger later.

## What people actually trust

In most teams and families, people trust leaders who are:

- **Honest about what they know and don't.**
- **Clear about how they'll find out.**
- **Consistent in how they treat people when things are uncertain.**

Confidence matters. False confidence erodes it.

## A useful sentence

*"I don't know yet. Here's how I'll find out, and when I'll come back to you."*

It's honest, it shows direction and it gives people something to rely on. It tends to build more trust than a confident guess that turns out wrong.

## Do the work between "I don't know" and the answer

1. **Clarify the question.** What exactly needs deciding?
2. **Gather what's missing.** Who knows? What data exists?
3. **Consult.** Ask the people closest to the problem.
4. **Decide and explain.** Share the reasoning, not just the verdict.
5. **Revisit.** Say so if the situation changes.

## Be consistent in how you handle uncertainty

People read your tone more than your facts. Stay steady, avoid blame and make it safe for others to say what they don't know too.

## For personal finances

The same applies at home. You won't always know the right move on investments, protection or a family decision. It's fine to say so, and to ask for advice and take time.

## Practise

The next time you're asked something you can't answer, use the sentence. Then do exactly what you said.

Authority built on honesty lasts longer than authority built on performance.
