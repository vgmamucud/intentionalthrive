Most of us can remember a moment, often small, that taught us something about money before we had the words for it.

A parent's face when a bill arrived. A treat you weren't allowed to ask for. Being given a coin and told to save it. Hearing a whispered argument from another room.

That memory is rarely just a memory. It's often the first line of the story you still tell yourself about money.

## Why it matters now

Early experiences set defaults. They shape what feels safe, what feels shameful and what feels normal, long before we make any adult decisions. You can earn a good salary and still react to a bill the way a child reacts to a closed door.

## The exercise

Give yourself ten minutes and a notebook.

1. **Write the earliest money memory you have.** Who was there? What did you see, hear, feel?
2. **Write what you decided about money in that moment.** Not what you were told, what you concluded. "Money causes fights." "We don't ask." "There's never enough."
3. **Write where that conclusion shows up now.** In spending, saving, avoiding, giving.
4. **Ask whether it's still true.** For you, now, with what you know.

## Be gentle

You're not looking for someone to blame. The adults around you were doing their best with what they had. You're only noticing the lesson you drew as a child, and deciding whether to keep it.

## What often changes

Seeing the memory takes some of the charge out of it. The urge to hide a statement, or overspend after stress, or refuse help, becomes less mysterious and a little more manageable.

You can honour what the memory protected you from and still choose differently.
