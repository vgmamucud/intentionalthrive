Most of us reach for the phone before we've fully opened our eyes. Within a minute we're inside other people's messages, other people's news and other people's urgency.

None of it is necessarily bad. But it means the day begins in reaction, not in intention.

## Why the first minutes matter

The start of the day is when you have the most say over where your attention goes. Hand it over immediately, and you spend the rest of the day taking it back.

## A small, realistic change

You don't need a perfect morning routine. You need a short buffer between waking up and the world arriving. Twenty minutes is plenty. Ten is a start.

## Build your own version

Choose two or three of these:

- **Water and light.** A glass of water and a minute at the window.
- **Movement.** A short stretch or a walk around the block.
- **Quiet.** Coffee, a prayer, or simply sitting without input.
- **One written line.** What matters most today?
- **A look at the day.** Open the calendar and see what's actually there.

## Make it easy to keep

**Charge the phone across the room.** If it's not beside the bed, it's not the first thing you touch.

**Use a real alarm clock** or put the phone's alarm out of reach.

**Decide the first task** the night before, so you don't have to choose while groggy.

## For busy households

If you have small children or others who need you early, your twenty minutes might be five. Take five. Even a brief moment that belongs to you changes how the day feels.

## What happens over time

You make fewer reactive choices. You notice earlier when something needs attention. And the day tends to feel less like something that happens to you.

Try it for one week and notice what's different. Start tomorrow, even if tomorrow's version is three minutes long.
