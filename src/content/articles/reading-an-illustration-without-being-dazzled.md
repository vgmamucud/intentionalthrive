An illustration is the document that shows how a plan is expected to behave over the years. It is full of numbers, usually arranged to tell a story. Learning to read it calmly is one of the most valuable financial skills you can build.

## First: what kind of numbers are these?

Every figure falls into one of three categories:

- **Guaranteed:** the contract commits to it.
- **Projected:** based on assumed rates of return or dividends. Possible, not promised.
- **Illustrative:** an example at a particular scenario.

Find the label on each column. If you can't tell which category a number belongs to, ask.

## The columns that matter most

**1. Total premiums paid, cumulative.** What you've put in by each year.

**2. Surrender or cash value, guaranteed.** What you'd receive if you stopped, using only the committed figures.

**3. Surrender or cash value, projected.** The same, under the assumed scenario.

**4. Death benefit,** and whether it changes over time.

**5. Charges and deductions,** where shown.

Compare columns 1 and 2 in the early years. Many plans show a gap, and that gap is the cost of leaving early.

## Questions to ask

- What rate of return is assumed? What happens at a lower one?
- Which figures drop if returns are poor?
- What are the charges, and do they change with age?
- What happens if I miss payments?
- What happens to the protection if the fund underperforms?

## Warning signs

- Only the highest scenario is shown.
- The conversation avoids the guaranteed columns.
- Time pressure to sign.
- Vague answers on charges.

## Practice

Take any illustration you own. Circle where the guaranteed value equals total premiums paid. That's the break-even year. Is it earlier or later than you expected?
