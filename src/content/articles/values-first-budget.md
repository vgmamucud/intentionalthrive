Most budgets begin with categories and limits. They tell you what you can't do.

A values-first budget begins with what you care about, and then checks whether your money agrees.

## Step 1: Name your values

Write five things that make your life good. Not what sounds impressive. What's true. For example: family time, health, learning, faith, generosity, security, adventure.

## Step 2: Map your spending to them

Take the last three months of statements. Next to each major expense, write which value it supports. If it supports none, mark it.

Some will surprise you. You might discover you value health but spend almost nothing on it, or that a recurring expense supports nothing you care about.

## Step 3: Look at the gap

Compare:

- **Where money goes** against
- **What you said matters.**

The gap is not a failure. It's information.

## Step 4: Adjust gently

Don't overhaul everything. Choose two moves:

1. **Increase** spending on one value that's underfunded.
2. **Reduce** one category that supports none.

Small changes you can sustain beat dramatic ones you abandon.

## Include the invisible values

Security is a value. So is the ability to rest. Premiums, emergency savings and retirement contributions belong on the list. They're how your values stay protected in a bad year.

## A sample

If your values are family, health and security:

- Family: shared meals, time off, support for parents.
- Health: gym or walks, regular checkups, health cover.
- Security: emergency fund, protection, steady investments.

The rest is flexible.

## Review it

Revisit every quarter. Values shift as life does, and your budget should shift with them. It pairs well with [the quarterly life review](/blog/the-quarterly-life-review).

A budget isn't a restraint. It's a statement of what you've decided is worth your effort.
