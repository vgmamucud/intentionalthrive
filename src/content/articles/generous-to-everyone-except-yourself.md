There's a word we use that doesn't translate cleanly into English: *utang na loob*.

It means a debt of the inner self — an obligation rooted not in money, but in gratitude. When someone helped you, you carry that. When family sacrificed for you, you carry that too. And the repayment isn't transactional. It's ongoing. It's cultural. It's woven into how we understand what it means to be a good person.

This is one of the most beautiful things about Filipino culture.

It's also, for many professionals, one of the most financially costly.

## The generosity paradox

Here's what I've observed: the most financially generous people I know are often the least financially protected.

They give freely. They lend without asking for repayment. They cover expenses for family members without being asked. They upgrade their parents' home before they build their own emergency fund. They say yes to everyone's needs while quietly pushing their own financial goals to "later."

And they do all of this not because they're reckless — but because they genuinely believe it's the right thing to do.

I'm not here to tell them they're wrong. The impulse to give is not the problem.

The problem is the belief, buried underneath all of that giving, that their own financial security is somehow less urgent, less deserving, less valid than everyone else's.

## Where this comes from

Most of us inherited a financial hierarchy without realizing it.

At the top: the needs of parents and elders. Then siblings, extended family, friends in crisis. Then the household. Then, somewhere at the bottom of the list — if there's anything left — your own future.

This hierarchy was never written down. It was modeled. Lived. Absorbed through watching the adults around us make choices about money, sacrifice, and what it means to love your family well.

It produced generations of people who are excellent at caring for others and deeply uncomfortable caring for themselves.

I know because I've lived it. And I've sat across from hundreds of clients who are living it right now.

## What it actually costs

The financial cost of this pattern isn't just what you give away. It's what you never build.

Every peso that goes to someone else's emergency — because you didn't have one — is a peso that didn't compound. Every year you delay your own insurance, your own investment, your own protection, because the money went elsewhere, is a year of growth you won't get back.

And here's what makes it harder: you don't feel bad about it. You feel good. Because giving feels like love, and love feels right.

But here's what I've come to believe after years in this industry:

You cannot sustainably give from an empty account.

The most generous thing you can do for the people you love is to become financially stable enough that *your* crisis doesn't become *their* crisis.

When you have no emergency fund and something goes wrong, you become the burden you were trying to prevent.
When you have no life insurance and something happens to you, the family you were protecting is left with nothing.
When you delay your retirement planning, you become dependent on the same children you sacrificed for.

Protecting yourself is not selfishness. It is the long game of generosity.

## The reframe that changes everything

I'm not asking you to stop giving. I'm asking you to change the order.

Before the next family request. Before the next "utang" you absorb without being asked. Before the next financial sacrifice that feels like love —

Ask yourself: *"Is my own oxygen mask on?"*

Do you have three months of expenses saved?
Do you have coverage for your income if something happens?
Are you building something that will last beyond your ability to work?

If the answer is no — that is the first place your money needs to go.

Not because you matter more than your family. But because your financial stability *is* your gift to your family. It's the one they'll need most when everything else falls apart.

## A note to those who feel guilty reading this

That guilt is the pattern talking.

The same conditioning that taught you to give everything is now making you feel wrong for wanting to keep some.

You are allowed to have savings.
You are allowed to say no to a request when it compromises your own foundation.
You are allowed to build wealth — for yourself, and because of that, for everyone you love.

Generosity that destroys you is not generosity. It's martyrdom.

And you deserve more than that.
